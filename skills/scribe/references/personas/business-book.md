# Persona: Business Book

For: business books, leadership books, productivity books, startup memoirs, management theory, applied behavioral economics for business audiences.

Audience: founders, executives, MBA students, knowledge workers in management roles. They will skim. They are looking for frameworks, principles, and stories they can quote in meetings.

This is the highest-AI-default-risk persona. Every business book is being trained on by every model. Most contemporary business books were already AI-default before AI: filler anecdote, bolded "Key Takeaway," three-bullet recap, repeat. AI tools turned this from a genre tendency into an irrefutable template.

The scribe goal for this persona is not to make business books literary. It's to make them readable — to get a business book past a sophisticated reader without triggering the "this is AI slop" gag reflex by page three.

---

## Core Voice

Specific. Anchored in a real company, executive, or moment. Refuses generic management theory. Uses one good story instead of three weak ones.

The successful business book voice has a personal stake — the author is reporting from inside an industry, not synthesizing what other business books said about it. AI-default business prose is meta-summary: a synthesis of synthesis, with no first-hand observation.

Think: Ben Horowitz (*The Hard Thing About Hard Things*), Patrick Radden Keefe (when writing about business — *Empire of Pain*), Brad Stone (*The Everything Store*), Ron Chernow (*Titan*), Liz Wiseman, Jim Collins (*Good to Great*), Andrew Grove (*Only the Paranoid Survive*), Phil Knight (*Shoe Dog*).

---

## Rhythm Rules

- **Open chapters with a scene, not a principle.** The CEO is in a meeting on March 14, 2003. The first sentence puts the reader in that room. The principle emerges from the scene by paragraph three or four.
- **Anecdote-density should be high but not gratuitous.** A real business book grounds every claim in a specific named example. AI-default business prose alternates principle, vague example, principle, vague example.
- **Bullet lists are allowed but expensive.** Every bullet list is a moment the reader stops experiencing prose and starts skimming. Use them when the items are genuinely parallel; use them sparingly.
- **No bolded "Key Takeaway" boxes.** This is the most explicit AI-default-business-book signature. Remove on sight.

## Vocabulary

- **Use real titles, real companies, real years.** "The CFO" beats "an executive." "Stripe in 2014" beats "a fast-growing startup." "Q3 2009" beats "the year the company was struggling."
- **Banned without context:** "synergy," "leverage" (as verb), "ecosystem," "stakeholder," "transformation," "best-in-class," "world-class," "thought leader," "disruptive," "frictionless," "actionable," "scalable" (used loosely), "operational excellence," "value creation," "strategic" (used as adjective for everything), "alignment," "drive results."
- **Avoid the consultant register.** McKinsey-speak is the gravitational center of bad business writing. The closer the prose drifts to deck language, the worse it reads.
- **Specific verbs.** "She wrote the term sheet" beats "she handled the negotiation." "He fired three vice presidents" beats "he restructured the leadership team."

## Forbidden Openers

| Forbidden | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| "In today's rapidly changing business landscape..." | Self-parody. |
| "What if I told you..." | Salesperson voice. |
| "Successful leaders all share one thing in common..." | They don't, and the reader knows it. |
| "Imagine you're the CEO of..." | The reader cannot. Show the CEO. |
| "The world of [industry] is undergoing a transformation..." | Cut. |
| "Every entrepreneur knows the feeling of..." | Universalizing. |
| "There's a reason why..." | Setup for a cliché. |

## Em-Dash Discipline

Business books are especially prone to em-dash overuse because the genre uses parenthetical asides and emphatic interruptions. Use the em-dash for genuine interruption only. Maximum 2 per page. Replace half with commas, half with periods.

## Persona-Specific Anti-Patterns (PSI-BB-*)

**PSI-BB-1: The Three-Person Anecdote Trio.**
"Take Sarah, a marketing director at a Fortune 500 company. Or consider Marcus, the founder of a successful tech startup. Then there's Amelia, a senior consultant..." Three flimsy anecdotes presented as a montage. AI-default. Real business books pick ONE story and stay with it for three pages.

**PSI-BB-2: The "And here's the thing" turn.**
After setting up a problem: "And here's the thing — [revelatory observation]." This rhythm is the contemporary business-book signature and AI tools default to it. Cut the literal phrase. If the structure works, the turn doesn't need announcing.

**PSI-BB-3: Made-up framework with a forced acronym.**
"I call this the FOCUS framework: Find, Observe, Calibrate, Update, Synthesize." The acronym is a tell that the framework was reverse-engineered from the chapter title. Skip the acronym.

**PSI-BB-4: The chapter-end summary box.**
"Key Takeaways: ✓ Leaders should..., ✓ Companies that..." Cut on sight. This is the surest AI-default tell in the genre.

**PSI-BB-5: The motivational closing chapter.**
The last chapter that pivots from analysis to "and now you can apply this in your own life." Most great business books end with their darkest case study, not with a how-to. The how-to belongs to a workbook or appendix, not the spine.

**PSI-BB-6: Fictional composite characters.**
"Meet Jordan, an SVP at a fictional company we'll call ACME Corp..." The fictional executive is a synthetic stand-in for analysis the author didn't do. Real business books name real people or stay abstract.

**PSI-BB-7: The "Don't believe me?" rhetorical move.**
"Don't believe me? Look at Microsoft." The reader doesn't disbelieve. The author shouldn't pre-fight.

## Signature Moves

1. **A first-paragraph scene with a real CEO making a real decision in a specific room on a specific day.** "It was 2:14 a.m. on October 28, 2008, when Brian Chesky walked into the Airbnb office. The site was down again."

2. **A specific number with scale context.** "Stripe processed $640 billion in 2022 — roughly the GDP of Sweden — but had only 7,000 employees."

3. **A counter-intuitive observation supported by a single deeply-reported case.** Not three thin anecdotes. One thick story that proves the point.

4. **An admission of what the framework doesn't solve.** "This works in software companies. It does not work in regulated industries; the FDA approval timeline alone breaks the cadence." Specifying the boundary builds trust.

## Exemplars

- **Ben Horowitz, *The Hard Thing About Hard Things*** — first-person scenes from inside Loudcloud / Opsware, no synthesis
- **Phil Knight, *Shoe Dog*** — memoir-of-business; specific scenes; refuses generic management voice
- **Brad Stone, *The Everything Store*** — Amazon scenes with named executives, dates, specific decisions
- **Andrew Grove, *Only the Paranoid Survive*** — strategic clarity, named tradeoffs, no synergy-speak
- **Patrick Radden Keefe, *Empire of Pain*** — Sackler family / Purdue scene-building, voice that refuses corporate flattery
